# Pedagogy and contrast

Use this reference for grammar lessons and any concept best learned through differences.

## Core principle

Preserve the context, change one instructional variable, let learners notice the difference, and reveal the rule only after the evidence.

Do not default to `explanation + illustration`. Choose the instructional move first, then the layout.

## Contrast map

Before building screens, record:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Known form | What learners already recognize |
| Target form | The new form or meaning |
| Constants | Words, subject, topic, character, and setting to preserve |
| Change | The smallest form or meaning difference to expose |
| Trigger | Time expression, situation, speaker intention, or reference point |
| Meaning contrast | What each option communicates |
| Common confusion | The likely incorrect generalization |
| Discovery question | A short prompt that directs attention without stating the rule |

If several variables change at once, split the comparison across screens.

## Default grammar rhythm

1. **Context:** establish a familiar situation.
2. **Notice:** show examples without explanation or answer styling.
3. **Compare:** highlight only the changed tokens or semantic cues.
4. **Discover:** reveal the concise rule after a learner observation.
5. **Check meaning:** use a CCQ or discrimination task.
6. **Check form:** use ordering, transformation, or error detection.
7. **Produce:** ask learners to create a new example in a fresh context.

Not every lesson needs all seven stages, but evidence must precede the completed rule.

## Two-picture semantic pair

Use two images when distinct scenes can make the grammar meanings easier to remember.

- Give both images equal size and mirrored card structure.
- Keep the same character and visual style whenever practical.
- Preserve the shared topic and change only the semantic cue.
- Pair each image with one complete learner-readable sentence.
- Keep the sentences as close to a minimal pair as grammar permits.
- Put context/time badges in HTML, not in the raster image.
- Keep images free of text, letters, numbers, labels, logos, and watermarks.
- Use image generation for meaning-rich scenes; use HTML for exact form and interaction.

Example:

| Finished past event | Life experience |
|---|---|
| Same learner shown during a completed Da Lat trip | Same learner now recalling or sharing that trip |
| `I visited Da Lat in 2024.` | `I have visited Da Lat before.` |
| Specific finished time | Experience; finished time not stated |

The first state should show both images and plain sentences. A later state may highlight `visited ... in 2024` and `have visited ... before`. Reveal the rule after learners answer a prompt such as `Which sentence gives a finished time?`

## Screen archetypes

### Context contrast

Use two semantic images and two minimal-pair sentences. Best for tense meanings, ability, obligation, real/imaginary situations, and completed/ongoing events.

### Form switch

Keep one sentence and interactively transform only the target tokens. Best for affirmative/negative/question forms and auxiliary changes.

### Mirrored comparison grid

Use equal columns with identical internal order: image, sentence, meaning cue, form cue. Best for two tenses or two related structures.

### Error repair

Show the unmarked sentence first. Let learners identify the repair location, then reveal the correction and reason.

### Rule synthesis

Collect two or three observed changes into one compact rule. Do not introduce unrelated exceptions on the same screen.

## Interaction states

Use staged disclosure:

1. `observe`: examples and images only;
2. `compare`: changed tokens and semantic cues highlighted;
3. `explain`: concise rule or takeaway revealed;
4. `check`: learner selects, builds, or repairs an example.

Never use animation as the only indicator of a grammar difference. Preserve color, text labels, and accessible state descriptions.

## Density and hierarchy

- Give example sentences and their contrast 60–70% of the visual attention on grammar-discovery screens.
- Keep explanation to one or two short lines after discovery.
- Avoid long paragraphs beside an illustration.
- Highlight the smallest meaningful token group, not the whole sentence.
- Reuse the same color for the same grammatical role throughout a lesson.
